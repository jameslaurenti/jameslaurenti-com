1. The short version

If you have followed Beverly over the last two years, you have probably seen some version of this list. Teachers went on strike in November 2024. The city's one municipal bus line was cut. Hours came down at the Beverly Farms library. On the first of July the trash fee went from $100 a year to $300.

Most people encounter these as four separate arguments, each with its own villain. They are closer to four symptoms of one problem, and the problem is worth understanding before anyone assigns blame.

A Massachusetts city cannot raise whatever it needs. State law caps the total it may collect in property taxes and lets that cap rise two and a half percent a year. Its biggest costs are set by other people and do not grow at two and a half percent. Health insurance rose about 9% this year. The pension assessment rose 4.5% a year for a decade on a schedule the state approves. The bill for the regional vocational school rose 13.7%. The city has to balance its budget anyway, every year, so whatever the cap does not cover has to be found by cutting something, charging for something, or both. Beverly's own forecast puts the shortfall at $7.1 million in 2028 and $13.7 million by 2030.1 Those are running totals rather than separate holes. Each year's figure contains the years before it, so what a new year adds is a little over $3 million.

That is where the list at the top comes from. The bus line and the library hours were cuts. The trash fee was a charge. The strike was about what years of the same arithmetic had already done to the schools.

None of that is unusual right now. Most communities in Massachusetts are in the same position, with the same costs pulling away from the same cap, and section 7 shows how many of them have reached the same decision in the same three years. On almost every measure the state publishes, Beverly sits in the middle of the pack.

What is unusual is how Beverly has handled it. There are two ways a community can vote itself more property tax than the cap allows, and both are decided by residents at a ballot box rather than by a mayor or a council.

The first is an override. It permanently lifts the cap, so the higher amount stays in the base and keeps growing two and a half percent a year afterward. It is how a community pays for recurring things: teachers, police shifts, library hours. Once it passes it never has to be renewed and it never expires. Medford passed the first override in its history in November 2024, $7.5 million for schools and roads, which added about $446 a year to the tax bill on an average Medford home.

The second is a debt exclusion. It is the temporary version, attached to one specific borrowing, usually a school or a fire station. It raises the tax bill for as long as that particular loan is being paid off, then comes off. Salem approved one in May of this year to build a new high school, adding about $534 a year to the median Salem bill, starting next spring and running for thirty years.

In at least thirty-six years Beverly has held neither vote. Not one of either kind.

Eighteen of the 351 Massachusetts municipalities can say that, and at first glance Beverly is just one of them. It is not, because the other seventeen all have something large paying the bills instead. Ten of them draw 20% or more of their revenue from the state, which Beverly does not; its share is below the state median. Five more have a commercial tax base at least 50% bigger than Beverly's. Quincy borrows on a scale Beverly does not, spending nearly four times as much of its budget on debt payments. Russell has 1,643 residents. That accounts for all seventeen, and Beverly is the only municipality in Massachusetts that has never asked and has no structural reason not to. Appendix A shows the arithmetic.

It has done that while keeping an ordinary amount of debt, an ordinary amount of cash on hand, below-average help from the state, and savings larger than 80% of Massachusetts communities hold. A city managing its money badly could not have done all four at once.

It cost something, and the cost landed on the schools. Beverly has funded them below the state median every year since 2013, reaching the 20th percentile in 2022. Since the strike it has closed most of that gap with its own money, moving from the 20th percentile to the 41st in four years. The city was not starving its schools on purpose, and section 4 explains how it ended up there anyway.

The sections that follow take each piece in turn. Where the squeeze comes from, why the schools were the exception, whether the spending decisions people argue about explain any of it, what the city is doing now, and what other communities have done on reaching this same point. Almost all of them eventually asked their voters for money. Beverly is one of the last that has not.

What to take from the chart: both lines climb, and the money coming in climbs more slowly. The shaded wedge between them is the gap the city has to close every year by doing something, and it gets wider each year rather than staying the same size.

The rest of this section is about why the wedge opens: where Beverly's money comes from, what limits it, and what happens to it once it arrives.

Where the money comes from

Almost all of it is property tax.

What to take from the chart: three quarters of everything Beverly spends comes from one source. Which means almost every question about the city's budget is a question about the property tax.

Are Beverly's taxes high

It depends which vantage point you are looking from, and the two answers point in opposite directions.

Compare towns and Beverly is cheap. Take the same $500,000 house and put it in different communities. Here it would be taxed $5,405. In the median community of Beverly's size it would be taxed $6,045, about $640 more. Beverly's rate is lower than roughly three quarters of them.

Look at your own bill and Beverly is not cheap. The average single-family tax bill here is $8,834 a year, higher than in two thirds of Massachusetts communities.2

House prices reconcile the two. A lower rate on a more expensive house still produces a bigger bill. Beverly's rate is low. Beverly's bills are not. If you are arguing with a neighbor about whether taxes here are high, you are probably both right and answering different questions.3

The rule underneath all of it

None of that decides how much the city collects in total. That is set by Proposition 2½, and it is the single most important rule in Massachusetts municipal finance.

You will hear the total collected from every property in the city put together called the levy. Proposition 2½ says the levy can rise 2.5% a year. Not 2.5% per household, and not 2.5% on any one bill: 2.5% on the whole city's collection. Individual bills move around inside that total as property values shift, which is why your own bill can rise more than 2.5% in a year the city's total rose exactly 2.5%.4

There is one exception to the 2.5%, and it is the reason development matters so much here. New construction is added to the cap on top of the 2.5%, automatically, with nobody voting on it. Say a year's worth of new building generates $1 million in property taxes. That million is added to what Beverly may collect and it stays there permanently, growing 2.5% a year afterward like everything else. Nineteen years of development has added roughly $31 million a year to what the city may collect, close to a quarter of the current cap.

Can Beverly build its way out

Some residents think the answer to all of this is to build more, and it is worth a careful answer, because building is the one lever that raises money without raising anyone's bill.

Set the destination at 2030. The city expects to collect $194.3 million that year and to need $208.0 million. The gap is $13.7 million. The question is how much of that building can close.

The state measures building pace as the new construction a community adds to its levy each year, against what it already collects. Beverly runs at 1.38%, the 44th percentile, close to the middle of the pack. Everything below is measured against that and assumes the faster pace is held every year from now to 2030, because one good year is not enough.

Two things sit behind that table.

The first is that a city cannot decide to build faster and then do it. Watertown is fifth of 196 communities of Beverly's size on this measure, and it got there over twenty years: between 2005 and 2024 commercial property went from 18.8% of its tax base to 28.7%. Over exactly the same years Beverly's went from 12.4% to 12.2%, up and down and back to where it started. The distance between the two is not a few more approvals. It is a difference in what each city has been doing with its zoning, its planning staff and its downtown for two decades, and that is not something a vote next spring can change.

The second is timing. New construction arrives one building at a time and then compounds, so a permit issued today does almost nothing for next year's budget. Development is the strongest tool Beverly has for changing its financial position over time, and it is close to useless against the gap that arrives next July. That combination, real money that takes years to arrive, is worth holding onto. It comes back at the end of this piece.

4. The schools, and what catching up cost

There was enough heat in this to produce a strike, and section 3 has already shown the city spending $14 million more to answer it. What that money was fixing takes some unpacking, because three different things all get called school funding and mixing them up is where most of the local argument goes wrong. They work like a formula: what the state sends, plus what the city adds, equals what reaches a classroom.

What the state sends. The state sets a minimum for every district, calculated from enrollment and student need, and it pays part of that minimum itself. Two things matter about it. The minimum is a floor, not a target: essentially every district in Massachusetts spends above it, and the typical one spends 36% above. And the state's share of Beverly's floor is small.

What the city adds. Beverly covers the rest of the floor, which it is legally required to do, and then whatever it chooses to put in on top.

What reaches a classroom. The two added together. Here is all of it in one picture.

What to take from the chart: the state pays $3,533 per pupil toward Beverly's minimum. It pays $5,428 toward the median district's. Beverly's own taxpayers more than make up that $1,895 difference, and the schools still end up $663 per pupil short of the median.

Two things are true at once here, and the local argument usually collapses them.

Measured the way the state measures it, as spending against the minimum it requires, Beverly has sat close to the state median for a decade. But measured in actual dollars out of local taxes, Beverly's residents have put in more per pupil than the typical Massachusetts district every year measured: $1,127 more in 2016, $758 more in 2024, $1,924 more now.

What has not been true is the outcome. The state pays Beverly roughly $2,000 less per pupil than it pays the typical district, every year, and local money did not cover the whole difference. Measured in dollars spent per student, Beverly has run below the state median every year since 2013 and reached the 20th percentile in 2022, the year the aid gap was widest and the local premium narrowest.

Whether the formula is fair, and whether average effort was enough, are both real arguments this piece does not settle. What the combination produced is not in dispute, and it showed up in two places a family notices.

The first is crowding. Beverly sat in the most crowded quarter of Massachusetts districts for eight straight years, from 2015 through 2022, peaking at the 88th percentile. That was one of several things the 2024 strike was about, alongside pay, benefits and support for students with disabilities, and it is the one the spending record shows most clearly. It had improved a good deal by then, from 14.8 students per teacher in 2015 to 11.9 in 2024.8 Two things to know about that improvement. It was built on the largest one-year staffing increase in the district's record, in 2023, much of it paid for with temporary federal pandemic money. And it has since partly reversed: teaching staff is down from 378 to 354 and the ratio is back to 12.7 this school year.

The second is pay. Teacher salaries were below the state median in eight of the ten years measured, bottoming at the 29th percentile in 2017, rising above the median in 2020 and 2021, and sitting at the 42nd in 2024. The sense that Beverly historically underpaid its teachers is supported by the record.

What changed after the strike, and who paid for it

Net school spending went from $77.7 million in 2024 to $90.0 million in 2026, up 15.8% in two years. Adjusted for inflation that is the largest two-year increase since 2002, and the second largest in the thirty-four years the state has published the series. It is why the percentile line in the table above turned.

Of that $12.3 million increase, $1.1 million came from the state and $11.1 million came from Beverly. Nine cents on the dollar and ninety one.

Hold that against the number this section opened with. In an ordinary year the state covers about 24 cents of every dollar in its own calculation of what Beverly's schools need. On the money it took to start closing the gap, it covered nine. The catching up is being done almost entirely by the community that fell behind, and that is a large part of why the budget is under pressure now.7

5. What people think is dragging the budget down

If the schools are where Beverly underspent, the obvious next question is whether it overspent somewhere else. That is the local suspicion, and it usually attaches to two projects: the City Hall renovation, and the purchase of 218-226 Cabot Street, the block with the old Family Dollar and the parking behind it. Both get read as evidence the city has been careless with money.

Start with the distinction the whole argument depends on. Some spending recurs and some does not. Salaries, insurance, heating oil and road salt come back every year forever. A building is bought once. Treating them as one pot is the most common mistake in this argument, because a one-time purchase cannot create a permanent hole.

How the city pays for something decides whether it shows up in the budget every year or only once.

Free cash is money left over when a year's books close. The important part is that it arrives once. It is closer to a tax refund than a raise. A refund is real money and you can do something useful with it, but if you use it to cover a monthly bill, the bill is still there next month and the refund is not.

Borrowing works the other way. The city takes a loan and the payments sit in the operating budget for the life of it, competing with everything else. The two projects need looking at separately, because they were paid for in opposite ways.

218-226 Cabot Street was $8 million authorized, funded mostly with one-time money: $4.5 million of free cash across two years, a $359,000 payment against principal, and a $2 million bond anticipation note, which the city has been retiring since. It does not recur.

City Hall is the one people want cancelled, so it is worth being exact about what it costs this year. The project is $29.4 million, of which about $4 million is cash and savings and $25.4 million is borrowed, in two loans. The first started in fiscal 2027 and its payment is about $2.0 million a year. That is the real number, and on its own it would be a serious addition to the budget.

It did not land that way, because the city timed it against loans that were ending. In the same year the first City Hall payment arrived, a land acquisition bond retired, a fire station loan retired, a one-time prepayment came off the books, and the rest of the portfolio shrank on its own schedule. Those together came to about $2.0 million of payments leaving. The net change in Beverly's total debt service was about $176,000. Both City Hall loans run at once from fiscal 2028, about $2.4 million a year, and the rest of the portfolio keeps shrinking underneath them. The fiscal 2027 piece walks through that schedule year by year.

You can think the renovation was the wrong call. What you cannot do is find the deficit inside it.

Now the broader charge, that Beverly has borrowed recklessly. Beverly's debt payments took 4.13 cents of every budget dollar in 2025, below the state median of 4.51 and at the 45th percentile. On the current numbers the borrowing is not out of line.

There is a version of this impression that fits the record, at least for a while. Debt payments took 5.7% of the budget in 2014 and climbed to 8.2% by 2019, staying above 6.5% every year through 2022 before falling to about 4% now. What drove the climb was the Briscoe Middle School project, which opened in 2018 with the state covering up to $49.2 million of eligible costs. Anyone looking at Beverly's books around 2019 was looking at a city carrying its heaviest debt load in a decade.

The interesting part is how Briscoe was paid for, and there is a comparison next door that makes it concrete. Most communities finance a school that size with a debt exclusion, and this May Salem did exactly that: a $447 million high school, $208 million of it from the state, the rest approved by Salem voters 4,093 to 3,028, adding about $534 a year to the median Salem tax bill for thirty years. Beverly built Briscoe without that vote. It absorbed the payments inside the existing levy, which meant five years of everything else in the budget competing against a school bond. That is the same pattern this whole piece is about, showing up in a single building.

These decisions did cost something, and it is not what people usually name. The $4.5 million of free cash spent on Cabot Street existed because Beverly consistently budgets less revenue than it collects, and the state's own guidance points that kind of surplus toward one-time uses like capital rather than toward operating costs. So the money went where the rules suggest. The real choice underneath is how conservatively to forecast in the first place, because a less cautious forecast would move some of that money into the operating budget where services are paid for. That is a live option, and section 6 is where the city is weighing it.

6. What the city is doing about it

Almost everything squeezing this budget was set somewhere other than Beverly. The cap comes from a 1980 ballot question. Health insurance is priced by a market. The pension schedule is written into state law and approved by a state agency. The aid formula is voted in Boston. Between them those four decide most of what the city collects and most of what it owes, and the city controls none of them.

The one large piece decided here is the school catch-up in section 4, and Beverly is paying for it deliberately. So there is no wasteful decision sitting on the list waiting to be reversed, which is what makes the next part hard.

The City Council created a deficit reduction committee by unanimous vote in March 2026, and it began meeting in July. It presents its list of ideas, with dollar amounts attached, on October 20, and takes it out to public meetings through December. Its agendas and minutes are posted by the City Clerk and later meetings are carried on BevCam, so residents can follow it in real time.

The search is wider than "find savings" suggests. The committee split its work across roughly nine areas that fall into three categories. There is revenue the city does not currently collect or collects lightly, including parking, municipal and rental fees, airport and field fees, and payments in lieu of taxes from large tax-exempt property owners. There is cost the city might restructure rather than cut, most notably health insurance. Part of that has already been examined and set aside. Beverly insures its own employees rather than buying coverage through the state's pool, and in September the finance director told the committee that a comparable state plan runs about $500 a year cheaper per person, which he judged too little to be worth giving up the reserves the city uses to absorb a bad year, in a switch that is close to one way. What is left is how much of the premium employees and retirees carry, which has to be bargained. And there is the ordinary category of departmental savings and shared services.

The health insurance thread is the one to watch, because it points at the line that did the most damage in section 3. It was the fastest growing part of the budget over seventeen years, so anything that bends that curve does more work than a comparable saving somewhere else.

One structural thing is worth knowing before the recommendations land. If what you picture when you hear "find savings" is trimming supplies, equipment and contracts, that pot is smaller than the problem. Set the school budget aside, which is about half the general fund and arrives as a single transfer. Everything the rest of the city buys that is not a person comes to about $12.2 million a year: $7.6 million of departmental expenses, the $2.7 million trash contract, $1.3 million of property and casualty insurance, and $0.6 million of capital outlay. The 2028 gap is $7.1 million, which is 58% of that. By 2030 the gap is larger than the entire pot, and a good deal of the pot is under contract anyway.

One thing cuts the other way, and it is worth holding next to that. The out-year numbers in the forecast are running totals. Every year in it grows from what the city asked for in 2027, and no year is ever assumed to be balanced, so the $7.1 million for 2028 contains the $3.9 million for 2027. That one was closed last June. What each new year adds is a little over $3 million. The distinction matters, because a hole widening from $3.9 million to $13.7 million reads as a city losing control, while the same numbers also describe a budget that falls about $3 million behind each year and has to make that up each year. Anything closed by something that recurs comes off every later year in the table, not just the year it lands in.

So the useful questions to bring to the October presentation are not whether the savings are real. Assume they are. Ask how big they are, and ask whether they come back next year.

A one-time saving of half a million dollars closes about seven percent of the 2028 gap and nothing at all the year after. A change that lowers what health insurance costs the city every year from now on is worth several times that, even if the first-year number looks smaller, because it bends a line rather than filling a hole once. The gap grows about $3.3 million a year. Anything that does not also grow is buying a single year.

Other communities have already worked through this list, which is where section 7 goes.

7. What comparable communities are doing

Comparable communities can be picked out on the five things that drive a municipal budget: household income, how much of the tax base is commercial, school enrollment, what the state calculates the district needs, and state aid per resident. Do that, and Beverly's twenty-nine closest are these.

Amesbury, Ashland, Barnstable, Bourne, Braintree, Chelmsford, Dartmouth, Dedham, Dracut, Easton, Falmouth, Gloucester, Grafton, Marshfield, Medford, Melrose, Natick, North Andover, North Attleborough, Northampton, Sandwich, Saugus, Shrewsbury, Somerset, Stoneham, Swansea, Tewksbury, Wakefield and Weymouth.

All twenty-nine have put a Proposition 2½ question to their voters. Beverly has never held a vote of any kind. Widen the circle to the sixty-two closest and sixty of those have asked.

The last three years are not a normal three years

If it seems like you have been hearing about override votes constantly, that is because you have.

What to take from the chart: override votes are not a steady background rate. They come in waves, and Massachusetts is in one now. Between 2020 and 2024 an average of eight municipalities a year put an operating override to voters. In 2026 it was thirty-two.

The quietest year in the entire thirty-eight-year record was 2023, when three communities held a vote. Three years later the number is ten times that. The last comparable stretch was 2005 through 2009, and before that the recession of the early 1990s.

That matters for how Beverly reads its own situation. The pressure did not build slowly and evenly and finally reach Beverly's door. It got sharply worse everywhere at once, in the years after the 2021 to 2023 inflation and the end of federal pandemic money, and communities that had gone decades without asking are arriving at the same conclusion in the same three years.

Three of them show what happens next.

North Attleborough shows what comes after one. It passed $6.5 million in 2019, its only override on record, and the money worked: the amount it was allowed to collect jumped 16.5% in a single year, against the 3.5% or so it grows in a normal one. Seven years later the pressure is back. Its 2027 budget carries a structural deficit of about $2 million, driven by health insurance rising near 20%.

What it did next is the part worth watching. It did not go back to voters. In June its Town Council voted nine to nothing to move employees and retirees onto the state's insurance plan from January 2027, which the town expects to save between $1.8 and $2.7 million a year, roughly the size of the deficit. Its manager put the alternative plainly: staying where it was would have meant putting $3 million more into the health insurance line. An override reset the level. Changing the insurance is an attempt at the slope.

It is worth noting why the same move is not worth as much to Beverly, which looked at it and passed. North Attleborough was facing an increase near 20% and a state plan priced well under what it was paying. Beverly's own plans are priced within about a percent of the comparable state plan, so the same switch buys it almost nothing. The lesson is not that one town was braver. It is that going after a cost is worth doing when the gap is large, and that how large the gap is has already been decided by things neither town controls.

Amesbury shows what happens when voters say no. Another Essex County city, and one that had never won an override in its history. In November 2025 it asked for $6 million and lost, 2,209 votes to 3,110. On the table had been 27 teaching positions and staff across police, fire, the Council on Aging and the library. The mayor read the result as an affordability problem.

What followed is the part that should look familiar. Amesbury made teaching cuts, and it raised parking and trash fees on top of them. Losing the vote did not end the need for the money; it changed how the city went after it. Beverly has just done a version of the same thing in its 2027 budget, cuts alongside a trash fee that tripled, without a vote ever being on the table.

Medford shows what happens when they say yes. In November 2024 Medford passed the first Proposition 2½ override in its history, on its first attempt, and it is the closest thing in this list to what Beverly would be doing. Two questions passed: $3.5 million for teacher salaries, school repairs, technology and a permanent three-person crew for roads and sidewalks, and $4 million more for the schools. Without them the city had been facing layoffs of teachers, school nurses and mental health staff. The cost was about $446 a year on an average home. A third question, a debt exclusion for a new fire headquarters, failed on the same ballot, which is worth noticing: voters did not say yes to everything on the ballot.

Beyond those three, Stoneham passed $9.3 million for 2027 after eight failed questions going back to 1991, and Ashland passed $2.75 million after thirty-six years of silence.

Put the three together and the lesson is not that an override fixes a budget. Across Massachusetts, forty-nine communities that were already collecting override money went back to their voters between 2025 and 2027, and thirty-one won again, a median of thirteen years after their last win. Those are communities that got the money and, over time, ended up in the same place. What separates them from North Attleborough is that North Attleborough went after a cost instead. Which is the difference the next section is about: what an override is, and what it buys.

8. What an override buys, and what it does not

An override permanently raises the cap, and the higher base then grows two and a half percent a year on top of itself. It is recurring money, which separates it from nearly everything else on the table.

What it does not do is change the arithmetic underneath. Look back at the second chart in section 3. An override lifts the revenue line. It does nothing to the angle of the cost lines above it. Health insurance keeps rising three times faster than the cap. Assessments from other agencies keep arriving. If none of that changes, a community that overrides arrives back at the same place some years later with a higher tax bill and the same problem.

So an override does not solve the problem. It buys years. How many depends entirely on what else changes, and the difference is large enough to draw.

What to take from the chart: a $10 million override in 2028, against a $7.1 million deficit, produces a surplus. What happens after that depends on the cost lines. If nothing else changes, the blue path, the surplus is gone by 2030 and by 2032 the deficit is larger than the one the override was meant to fix. If cost growth also slows, the green path, from the 4.6% a year the city projects down to 3.5%, the surplus lasts through 2031 and the 2035 gap is $5 million rather than $25 million. The vote buys the time. What is done with the time decides how much it was worth. Neither piece of pension news below is in this model: not the assessment holding flat, which takes roughly $0.7 million, $1.4 million and $2.1 million off the 2028, 2029 and 2030 points, and not the obligation clearing in 2032. Both push all three lines down.

So the useful question, the one worth bringing to any public meeting about this, is what the years would be for.

Something in that picture has to bend, and there is a short list of candidates. Some of them Beverly controls and some it does not.

One line has already bent, and nobody had to vote on it. Nearly every Massachusetts community is paying down an unfunded pension obligation on a schedule the state approves, and this is a normal feature of municipal budgets rather than a sign anything went wrong here. Beverly's schedule had that payment rising 4.5% a year. In September the city's retirement board adopted a new one that holds it flat instead, through 2029 at least, without moving the date the obligation clears. Measured against what the forecast assumed, that is about $674,000 in 2028 and $1.4 million in 2029, or roughly a fifth of what a typical year adds to the gap.9

Why it happened is the part worth sitting with. Beverly took those 4.5% increases early, before it had to, and they cost the budgets of those years something real. The money went in early enough to earn, two strong market years followed, and the city can now reach the same finish line without the increases. The finance director, who chairs the retirement board, put it to the committee as credit owed to the people who agreed to pay more sooner. Two things make it less fragile than it sounds. The accounting recognizes market gains in thirds, so gains already earned are still arriving. And the city may not by law pay less than it paid the year before, which means flat is the floor rather than a decision to go easy. What it is not is a lever anyone can pull again.

The obligation itself still ends, and that is the larger number. When the schedule clears, about $9 million a year stops leaving the operating budget, in two steps in 2032 and 2033. That is recurring money of roughly the size an override would raise. It is less than earlier schedules implied, because a flatter path has less to fall off. Treat the date as close rather than certain: the valuation is rebuilt every two years and Beverly's has moved in both directions before. It also arrives after the current forecast ends.

Development can bend the revenue line. Section 3 put numbers on it. Sustained building 50% faster than Beverly manages now would cover about a third of the 2030 gap. At Waltham's pace it would cover not quite half. Neither arrives in time to help next year, which is exactly what makes it a poor answer to an immediate deficit and a good one for a bridge.

Health insurance can bend the cost line, and the city is already looking at it. It is the fastest growing line in the budget, so a change there does more work than a change anywhere else. Section 6 has what the city has already ruled out and what is left, which is what share of the premium employees and retirees carry, and that has to be bargained rather than decided.

Shared services can bend it too, in the ordinary unglamorous way of two or three communities running one dispatch center or one purchasing operation. That is also on the committee's list.

And some of it is not Beverly's to decide. The state could fund another school aid expansion like the one that just ended, or restore what unrestricted aid has lost to inflation over twenty years. Federal policy could move either way. None of that can be put on a calendar.

So an override is a bridge, and how long it lasts depends on how many of those things bend while you are standing on it. A community that overrides and changes nothing else buys two years of relief and is worse off than when it started inside of four. A community that overrides while its development pipeline fills, its insurance costs moderate and its pension obligation retires is buying most of a decade, and may not need to come back at all. That is the difference between borrowing time and using it.

9. What kind of town

Here is the whole thing in one place.

Beverly is an ordinary Massachusetts city. It spends about what comparable communities spend, borrows an ordinary amount, keeps more in savings than 80% of communities hold, and charges one of the lower tax rates in Massachusetts. It has done that for thirty-six years without once putting a question on a ballot.

The pressure it is under is not of its own making and is not unique to it. A cap that rises two and a half percent a year cannot keep pace with health insurance, negotiated raises and assessments the city does not control, and the state money that used to cover the difference has been falling in real terms for twenty years.

Where Beverly did fall short, it was on the schools, and it has spent the last two years fixing that with local money because no other money arrived. It has moved its schools from the 20th percentile of the state to the 41st in four years, almost entirely at its own expense. That correction is a large part of the pressure the city now faces. It is also, on the evidence, the thing residents most wanted.

The same instinct paid off somewhere else. Beverly put money into its pension obligation faster than it had to, at a real cost to the budgets of those years, and that money has now earned enough that the payment stops rising while the obligation still clears on schedule. Pay early, hold back, bank the difference. That is one disposition, and it produced both results.

The city is now working through every remaining option: fees it does not charge, payments it might negotiate, insurance it might restructure, services it might share. Some of that will work. The test is whether it recurs and whether it scales.

Twenty-nine of the communities most like Beverly have already been here. Every one of them put the question to voters, and nine of them did it in the last three years, because the last three years have been the worst stretch for municipal budgets in Massachusetts since the 2008 financial crisis.

Which leaves the part nobody can do with a spreadsheet. On the arithmetic as it stands, and unless something in it changes, Beverly will in the next few years either raise more money from the people who live here, through a vote or through fees that arrive without one, or provide less: fewer teachers, fewer hours, longer waits, more things quietly not done. Some of what could change is listed in section 8, and some of it is plausible. One piece has already arrived without anyone voting on anything, and it is worth about a fifth of what a typical year adds. The rest of the levers that used to keep this decision invisible are used up.

That is a choice about what kind of place this is supposed to be, and it belongs to residents rather than to a forecast. It is worth deciding what you think before someone hands you a ballot.

The one that comes closest

Danvers is the nearest thing to a second Beverly and deserves saying out loud, because it is next door and the comparison will occur to people. It gets less state aid than Beverly, 10.0% against 13.3%. It holds less than half the reserve, 5.6% against 13.8%. And it builds more slowly, 0.85% against 1.38%, the second slowest on this list.

What Danvers has is business. Its commercial base is 20.9% against Beverly's 11.6%, its household income is 18% higher, and it runs a municipal electric utility, one of only forty in the state. Add it up and Danvers raises about $535 more per resident in general fund revenue than Beverly does, almost all of it from business and fees. At Beverly's population that difference would be worth roughly $21 million a year.

Danvers is not obviously comfortable. It is at its levy cap, it has a thinner cushion, and it builds less. What it has not had to do yet is choose.

Appendix B: how the comparison group was built

The twenty-nine communities in section 7 come from a single construction, so that every comparison in this piece uses the same definition of "like Beverly."

Every Massachusetts municipality with more than 8,000 residents that runs its own school district was scored on five measures: median household income, commercial share of the tax base, school enrollment, foundation budget per pupil (the state's own need-weighted estimate of what a district's students require), and state aid per resident. Each measure was scaled by its own statewide interquartile range so that no single one dominates, and distance to Beverly is the root mean square of the scaled differences. The twenty-nine closest are those within 0.50 on that scale; the sixty-two closest are those within 0.60.

Two things about the method are worth stating rather than hiding.

Membership is not stable. Drop any one of the five measures and roughly a quarter of the list changes, and dropping enrollment changes most of it. What does not move is the override record: at every band tested, from the three closest communities out to the sixty-two closest, essentially all of them have put a Proposition 2½ question to voters and Beverly has not. The argument in section 7 rests on that rate, not on the particular roster.

Marblehead, the comparison Beverly often reaches for, ranks 106th out of 176 on this construction. Same housing market, very different budget.
