# Beverly Under the Hood: A Driver Ledger and the Shape of a Long-Term Strategy

**Working draft · July 2026 · Companion to the FY27 Budget Explainer**

This document does three things. First, it breaks Beverly's budget into its individual revenue and cost drivers and gives each one a recent trajectory, a forward projection, a confidence level, and a list of what could move it. Second, it identifies which drivers Beverly actually controls, which it can influence, and which it can only absorb. Third, it backs into the strategy structure: the things Beverly should do regardless of any override, and what an override would buy, prevent, and cost while those things take effect.

Confidence key: **HIGH** = formula or contract, the number is close to knowable. **MED** = trend-driven, reasonable range. **LOW** = policy- or event-dependent, wide range.

Control key: **CITY** = Beverly decides. **NEGOTIATED** = Beverly is one party at a table. **STATE/MARKET** = Beverly absorbs.

## Part 1: Revenue Drivers

### 1.1 Levy base growth (the 2.5%)

| | |
|---|---|
| FY27 | +$3.39M |
| Recent | Grows mechanically each year as the base compounds. ~$135.6M levy implied by FY27 figures. |
| Forward | +$3.5M FY28, +$3.6M FY29, +$3.7M FY30. The one revenue line that never disappoints and never surprises. |
| Confidence | HIGH |
| Control | STATE (formula), unless voters change it via override |

**What could move it:** Only an override (up) or nothing (it cannot go down). Note the compounding property: every dollar of override or new growth added to the base earns 2.5% forever after. This is why early structural revenue is worth more than late structural revenue.

### 1.2 New growth — QUANTIFIED, 11-year series (DLS LA-13)

| | |
|---|---|
| FY27 | +$1.95M (budget book actual) |
| The series | New growth added to the levy limit, FY16–FY26: $843K, $1.31M, $2.19M, $1.70M, $1.85M, $1.40M, $1.37M, $1.56M, $2.10M, $1.53M, $1.60M. **11-year mean $1.59M; last-3-year mean $1.75M.** |
| Trend | Essentially flat with noise. Linear slope is +$33K/yr, statistically nothing. As a share of prior-year levy it runs ~1.2–1.3% lately, just above the 1.2% statewide median. Beverly is an average new-growth city, not a star and not a laggard. |
| Forward | On history alone, expect $1.5–2.0M/yr. The FY27 $1.95M is a good year, near the top of the range, not a new baseline. |
| Confidence | HIGH on the baseline (this is the actual series); LOW on whether a pipeline can lift it |
| Control | CITY (zoning, permitting, EDAP, disposition of city parcels) |

**The finding that reframes X1.** The Financial Forecast Committee budgets new growth at **$1.25M/yr**, well below the $1.59M 11-year actual mean. That is the Committee's deliberate conservatism (they underestimate revenue on purpose), but it means the city's official planning assumes new growth *below its own historical floor*. Two consequences: first, actual new growth almost always beats the forecast, which is part of why Beverly generates free cash. Second, and more important for strategy, there is real headroom between what the city plans for ($1.25M) and what a genuine development push could deliver.

**The commercial-mix angle, now with data.** Residential vs. CIP (commercial/industrial/personal) share of new growth swings year to year: FY24 was 72% CIP, FY26 was 48% CIP. This matters because CIP new growth is worth more to the city per dollar than residential. Commercial property pays taxes without adding kids to schools or heavy service demand, while residential new growth brings service costs with it (the FY27 explainer flags exactly this). A development strategy that tilts toward commercial/mixed-use isn't just bigger new growth, it's higher-margin new growth. The parcels you've flagged (Family Dollar/Cabot, River Street, Bass River TOD) should be evaluated on CIP yield, not just total value.

**What X1 has to beat:** the realistic target is lifting the sustained average from ~$1.6M to ~$2.5–3M/yr. That's not doubling; it's roughly one extra mid-sized commercial project per year on top of the normal churn. Each $1M of sustained new growth ≈ $1M/yr of permanent, compounding revenue (unlike the levy's 2.5%, new growth stacks on top and then itself grows at 2.5% forever after). Closing even half the wedge this way is plausible over 3–5 years, and it's the only lever that both compounds and doesn't cost residents anything.

### 1.4 PILOT payments — QUANTIFIED from the parcel data

| | |
|---|---|
| FY27 | $250K, unchanged for years |
| The real gap | Beverly's exempt base is **$1.01 billion** (8.7% of the city's $11.65B total assessed value), across 480 parcels. Strip out government/city/housing-authority parcels and the PILOT-eligible universe (private education + charitable + hospital) is **$451.6M assessed = ~$4.96M/yr in foregone taxes** at the $10.99 rate. |
| Forward | Flat by default. Moves only with a campaign. |
| Confidence | HIGH on the assessed values (FY26 parcel data); MED on what any negotiation yields |
| Control | NEGOTIATED |

**The institutions, by foregone tax (FY26 assessed values, $10.99/$1,000):**

| Institution | Parcels | Assessed value | Foregone tax/yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Endicott College | 17 | $196.1M | **$2.16M** |
| Beverly Hospital (Northeast Hospital Corp / Beth Israel Lahey) | 8 | $46.7M | $514K |
| Landmark School | 8 | $31.2M | $343K |
| Shore Country Day School | 4 | $19.5M | $214K |
| YMCA of the North Shore | 4 | $19.1M | $210K |
| Montserrat College of Art | 7 | $14.6M | $161K |

**The headline for the PILOT argument:** Endicott College alone forgoes **$2.16M/yr** in taxes. Beverly's total PILOT revenue from all sources is $250K, which is **12% of Endicott's foregone tax alone**, and Endicott is only one of dozens of exempt holders. The earlier "~$6M central estimate" for Endicott was high; the parcel data puts it at $2.16M, which is still nearly 9x the entire current PILOT take.

**What a PILOT program would yield (realistic bands):**
- Boston's model (25% of assessed-equivalent, half creditable as community benefits, so ~12.5% effective cash) applied across the full private-ed + charitable universe: **~$620K/yr**.
- Full 25% cash equivalent: **~$1.24M/yr**.
- Endicott alone at Boston's 25% gross: **~$539K/yr** (vs. its current contribution, which is bundled into the $250K citywide total).

So the honest PILOT ceiling is roughly **$600K–1.25M/yr recurring**, a meaningful dent in a $3–4M wedge, and by far the largest "someone other than residents" revenue lever available. The political value exceeds even that: it is the concrete answer to "why should homeowners pay more before a college sitting on $196M of untaxed property does." Full data in the exported CSVs (`beverly_exempt_parcels_FY26.csv`, `beverly_exempt_owners_summary.csv`).

**Leverage:** Endicott wants things from the city periodically (development approvals, zoning, road/utility coordination). Those are the moments a structured PILOT gets negotiated. Andover/Phillips Academy is the Essex County precedent. The other host communities (Salem with Salem State, etc.) are natural coalition partners for a legislative PILOT push.

| | |
|---|---|
| FY27 total health insurance | $13.22M (dept request), +9.0% over FY26 |

**What self-insured changes about the strategy.** Being self-insured is why the number moves 10% in a single year: Beverly absorbs its own claims volatility directly rather than smoothing it through GIC's 400,000-member pool. Two implications. First, the same GLP-1/specialty-drug spike that forced GIC to cut coverage hits Beverly's balance sheet with no buffer. Second, this is the one place Beverly might replicate Belmont's move: the FY27 explainer and the forecast both mention a "restructured prescription drug program for retirees and the City for significant savings" already done, which is exactly the kind of self-insured lever available. Evaluating GIC entry (as Belmont just did, fleeing a self-insured 24% spike) is a live, quantifiable option. **[REMAINING TASK: get Beverly's employee cost-share percentage and a GIC-entry comparison from Finance; the budget book confirms carrier and amount but not the split.]**

**What could move it:** Plan design changes; cost-share shift (Houseman's below-80% idea, worth ~$130K/yr per point on a $13M base); GIC entry evaluation; continued Rx restructuring. A 1–2 point trend reduction is the realistic ceiling of local action, ~$150–300K/yr compounding. None bends the underlying medical trend.

### 2.4 Pension contribution — RESOLVED, and it changes everything

Source: PERAC, Beverly Contributory Retirement System Actuarial Valuation, January 1, 2024 (schedule approved by PERAC October 2024). Beverly runs its own system; it is not in Essex Regional.

| | |
|---|---|
| Funded status (1/1/24) | 75.3% funded; $65.6M unfunded liability; 7.0% return assumption |
| FY27 | $15.58M total appropriation (+4.5%/yr per schedule; matches the budget book's +$669K) |
| Forward | The adopted schedule rises 4.5%/yr through FY31 ($18.58M), makes a final reduced amortization payment in FY32 ($11.74M), and then collapses to normal cost only in FY33: **$5.80M**. |
| Confidence | HIGH on the schedule as adopted; MED on the date holding (see risk below) |
| Control | ACTUARIAL until the cliff; CITY afterward |

The schedule, verbatim from the valuation:

| FY | Total appropriation | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | $15.58M | +4.5% |
| 2028 | $16.28M | +4.5% |
| 2029 | $17.02M | +4.5% |
| 2030 | $17.78M | +4.5% |
| 2031 | $18.58M | +4.5% |
| 2032 | $11.74M | **−36.8%** |
| 2033 | $5.80M | **−50.6%** |

**The cliff is FY2032–33, and it is worth roughly $12.8M/yr** (FY31 payment vs. FY33 payment), permanent, on a schedule PERAC has already approved. Until then the pension line is itself a wedge-widener, adding ~$700–800K/yr at 4.5% against 2.5% revenue growth. After FY33, that reverses into the largest single structural relief in the city's modern budget history.

**What could move the date:** Investment returns. The valuation's own stress test shows that at 3% annual returns the unfunded liability grows instead of shrinking and "the funding schedule might have to be increased before FY29." PERAC's stated outer bound is full funding by FY35, so the realistic worst case is a 2–3 year slip, not a decade. Also note the two-step shape: FY32 already releases ~$6.8M vs. FY31. Relief begins before full funding completes.

**The detail almost nobody has noticed:** the $50M Public Services Building enters the city's January 2026 debt schedule in FY33, the exact year the pension payment collapses. The pension release (~$12.8M/yr) covers the projected FY33 debt jump (+$4.8M) nearly three times over. Whether by design or coincidence, the city's capital planning is already implicitly spending the cliff. That is a fact worth surfacing publicly, because it means the freed capacity is contested before it exists: debt service, restored services, school funding, reserve rebuilding, and tax relief will all have claims on it. Deciding that allocation openly, in advance, is itself a civic project.

## Part 3: The Wedge, Reassembled — now from the city's own forecast

The December 2025 Financial Forecast Committee report (Feldman, chair) replaces my midpoints with the city's numbers. It is blunter than anything in the FY27 explainer. The Committee calls this deficit "qualitatively different" from prior years, notes that the strategies used to close past gaps "no longer exist," and states that closing FY27 "for the first time in decades requires raising fees/or cutting services with unprecedented impact."

The forecast's own five-year revenue and expenditure lines (general fund, operating budget only, enterprise funds excluded):

| ($M) | FY26 budget | FY27 | FY28 | FY29 | FY30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Estimated revenues | 173.72 | 178.87 | 183.78 | 189.07 | 194.32 |
| Estimated expenditures | 173.72 | 182.79 | 190.89 | 199.09 | 207.998 |
| **Surplus / (deficit)** | **0** | **(3.92)** | **(7.11)** | **(10.01)** | **(13.68)** |

This is the Marblehead shape in Beverly's own hand. Revenue grows about 2.9%/yr; expenditures grow about 5%/yr; the gap compounds from $3.9M to $13.7M over four years. Note this is the *pre-balancing* forecast: the FY27 explainer showed how the Administration closed the $3.9M for one year with the trash reprice and cuts. The forecast shows what returns, larger, once those one-time moves are spent.

The confidence note matters. The Committee uses a deliberately conservative method: revenues underestimated, expenditures estimated objectively (their words). So the revenue lines likely have modest positive variance built in, and actual deficits may run slightly smaller than shown. But the direction and slope are the city's official position, not my model.

**Driver-level detail the forecast confirms or corrects:**

- **Revenue growth is slowing.** The Committee flags FY27's 2.97% revenue increase as "less than prior years." New growth is budgeted at only **$1.25M** in the forecast (vs. the $1.95M actually realized in FY27), and the Committee assumes new growth of roughly $1.2M/yr flat through FY30. This is the conservative-forecasting convention, but it also means the city is not counting on a development pipeline. That is the gap X1 has to fill.
- **The levy is maxed.** "This will bring the City up to the levy limit, and there is no more tax levy revenue available." No excess levy capacity remains after FY27. That lever, used for $1.4M over the prior two years, is now empty.
- **Health insurance** grows at 6% per the forecast's assumption (line item detail below), from $15.51M (FY27) toward $19.6M (FY30). The screenshots confirm Beverly is **self-insured** (see 2.3, corrected).
- **Pensions** track the PERAC schedule exactly: the forecast shows retirement rising 4.5%/yr to $17.1M by FY30, matching the valuation. The forecast horizon ends at FY30 and so does not show the FY32–33 cliff. That is the single most important thing the city's own forecast omits, and the biggest analytical value-add available.
- **Vocational (Essex Tech)** is the fastest-growing line in percentage terms: +13.66% FY27, and the forecast assumes 3%/yr after, though it flags enrollment-driven volatility.
- **Reserves are being spent.** The forecast confirms ~$7M drawn from reserves before FY26 close (City Hall, roads, stabilization, OPEB transfer). Free cash certified at $10.5M (FY26), undesignated fund balance ~$14.2M. Healthy now, declining if operations keep drawing on them.

**The cliff, restored to the picture.** The city's forecast ends at FY30 showing a $13.7M deficit and no way out. Extend it two years on the same slopes and add the PERAC schedule, and FY32 releases ~$6.8M while FY33 releases ~$12.8M/yr. The forecast's terminal despair (FY30, −$13.7M, "no amount of expenditure trajectory can continue") is real, but it is also the darkest point on the curve. The pension relief lands 24–36 months past where the city's own document stops looking.

## Part 4: The Strategy, Backed Into

The structure requested: **do X regardless; an override prevents Y at cost Z.**

### X: Do these regardless of any override

Ranked by (expected annual value × controllability), with honest timelines:

**X1. Build the new growth engine.** The 11-year series sets the baseline at ~$1.6M/yr average (the city plans for only $1.25M). Target: lift the sustained average to ~$2.5–3M/yr through the identified parcels, EDAP, and pro-commercial zoning, roughly one extra mid-sized commercial project per year. Value: ~$1M/yr recurring per increment, compounding at 2.5% forever, and CIP growth is higher-margin than residential. Timeline: 2–4 years to revenue per project. Control: CITY. This is the lever Marblehead does not have.

**X2. Run a real PILOT campaign.** The parcel data makes this concrete: the private-ed + charitable + hospital exempt base is $451.6M assessed, ~$4.96M/yr in foregone taxes, with Endicott alone at $2.16M. A structured program (Boston's 25% framework) realistically yields **$600K–1.25M/yr recurring** against the current $250K. Timeline: 1–3 years of negotiation. Control: NEGOTIATED. The political value exceeds the dollars: it is the answer to "why should residents pay first," and no override passes without it.

**X3. Restructure health insurance.** Plan design + cost share + GIC/MIIA evaluation, via coalition bargaining. Value: $150K–$500K/yr, compounding, plus OPEB relief. Timeline: next open contract/plan cycle. Control: NEGOTIATED. This is the largest cost line the city can actually touch.

**X4. Plan against the FY32–33 pension cliff (date now confirmed).** PERAC's approved schedule releases ~$6.8M in FY32 and ~$12.8M/yr from FY33 onward versus the FY31 peak. Value: the endgame itself. The strategy question is no longer whether relief comes but who has already claimed it: the $50M Public Services Building lands on the debt schedule the same year. A public "cliff allocation" framework, set before FY30, is the highest-leverage governance move available.

**X5. Hold the capital and debt discipline.** Keep the flat-5% borrowing playbook; gate the $50M Public Services Building against the pension date and the operating outlook. Value: avoided ~$3M/yr of FY33+ pressure if deferred or downsized.

**X6. State advocacy portfolio.** UGGA reform, minimum-aid floor, circuit breaker full funding, Essex Tech member-community coalition. Individually low probability; collectively worth pursuing because the cost is staff time and the payoff is recurring. Expected value maybe $200–500K/yr across the portfolio, someday.

Realistic combined effect: X1–X3 shrink the annual wedge from ~$3M toward ~$1–1.5M within 3–4 years. They do not close it. X4 closes it, later, on a date. That gap between now and the structural fixes landing is precisely what an override is for.

### Y and Z: What an override prevents, and what it costs

**Without an override,** the city's own forecast is the Y: deficits of $3.9M (FY27), $7.1M (FY28), $10.0M (FY29), $13.7M (FY30), pre-balancing, closed each year only by the break-glass list the FY27 explainer already started drawing from. The Committee names the tools that "no longer exist": the roads/sidewalk shift to free cash ($2.7M, done), excess levy capacity ($1.4M, spent), the Rx restructuring (done). What remains is service and staff reduction. That is Y, and it is the city's word, not mine.

**The override that fits the math** is not Marblehead's $15M, and now the forecast lets us size it precisely. The bridge window is FY28–FY32. The cumulative pre-balancing gap over those years, on the forecast's own lines extended to FY32, is roughly $45–50M gross, but that overstates the ask: the levy still grows 2.5%/yr underneath, new growth still comes in, and the structural moves (X1–X3) chip at it. The override only needs to cover the portion that discipline and growth can't reach, and only until the FY32 pension step-down starts absorbing it. A phased operating override of roughly **$3–4M/yr over three years, ~$10–12M total**, is the realistic band, larger than my earlier $8–9M estimate because the forecast's deficits are steeper than my midpoints. Still well under Marblehead's $15M, and with a dated far shore Marblehead never had.

**Z, the cost to residents:** Beverly's FY27 levy is ~$135.6M (forecast: tax revenue net of overlay $139.6M including new growth). Median single-family bill ~$7,100–7,700 (FY25 rate $10.99/thousand; Census-reported median real estate tax paid $6,827–6,926). Each $1M of override ≈ 0.72% on the levy ≈ **~$50–55/yr on the median bill**. A $3.5M/yr phase ≈ ~$180/yr per step, ~$540/yr cumulative by year three. For context, Beverly's median household income is $106,044 (2024 ACS) and 53.4% of adults hold a bachelor's or higher, a demographic and fiscal profile that reads override-capable, closer to Arlington or Melrose (which pass them) than to a stressed Gateway City. Marblehead's comparable ask cost $1,230 cumulative with no end date. Beverly's would be roughly 40% of that, with a pension cliff visible on the horizon.

**The honest framing, which is also the winning framing:** Marblehead passed its override by proving discipline first and offering tiers. Beverly can do one better, because Beverly has what Marblehead lacks: a development pipeline, a PILOT counterparty worth real money, and a pension schedule with an end date. The ask is not "pay more forever because costs grow." The ask is "fund a bridge, with the far side visible: new growth compounding, institutions contributing, and the pension payment expiring." An override sold as a bridge to dated, named structural fixes is a different product than an override sold as a subscription. Marblehead sold a subscription and barely passed it. The bridge is easier to sell, and it has the advantage of being true, if and only if the city actually executes X while the bridge is up.

The failure mode is equally clear, and Marblehead will demonstrate it in real time by 2029: take the money, skip the structural work, and return to voters with a bigger number and less trust.
